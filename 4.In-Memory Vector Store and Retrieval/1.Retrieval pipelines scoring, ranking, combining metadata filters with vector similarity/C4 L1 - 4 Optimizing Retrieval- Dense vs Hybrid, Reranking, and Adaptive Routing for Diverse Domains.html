Why Your RAG Pipeline’s Retrieval Strategy Depends on the Query, Not Just the Domain
I benchmarked 6 retrieval configurations across 4 real-world domains. The results changed how I think about building retrieval systems.
When people talk about optimizing RAG pipelines, the conversation almost always ends up in the same place: “just add a reranker” or “hybrid retrieval is always better than BM25.” After spending several weeks building a reproducible retrieval benchmark - QueryAware-HybridRAG - I can tell you that both of those statements are wrong, or at least dangerously incomplete.
The real answer is more interesting: the best retrieval strategy depends on the query, not just the domain. And if you design your system around that insight, you can get near-optimal quality at a fraction of the latency cost.
Here’s what I found.

Finding 1: Dense Retrieval Beats Hybrid on Semantic Domains
The most counterintuitive result: on FiQA (personal finance), dense-only config B (MRR 0.4797) outperformed hybrid config C (MRR 0.3402) by 32%.
This seems wrong until you think about it carefully. FiQA queries are paraphrastic - the same financial concept gets phrased in many different ways. Dense embeddings capture that semantic intent well. BM25, on the other hand, relies on lexical overlap. When you fuse a strong dense signal with a weak BM25 signal, you don’t get the best of both worlds - you get noise, unless fusion weights are carefully tuned per domain.
The practical lesson: Don’t default to hybrid retrieval. On semantically rich domains, dense-only is both faster and more accurate.
Finding 2: Reranking Helps Most Where It’s Hardest to Retrieve
Cross-encoder reranking (configs D and E) produced its biggest gains on SciFact (+13% MRR over B) and NFCorpus (+16% MRR over B) - both technically dense domains with specialized vocabulary.
Here’s why: first-stage retrieval on technical domains often surfaces documents that are topically adjacent but not precisely relevant. A biomedical query about “mTOR pathway inhibition” might retrieve documents about cancer broadly, but not the specific mechanism paper. The cross-encoder fixes this by re-scoring every (query, candidate) pair with a full transformer forward pass - reading them together rather than comparing vectors independently.
But this comes at a real cost. Configs D and E are 3.5x slower than B on average, delivering only ~7.5% MRR gain cross-domain. The math only works in your favor on technically complex domains.
Why is reranking so expensive? First-stage retrieval uses approximate nearest neighbor search and inverted indexes - both designed for speed. The cross-encoder has no such shortcut. For a top-50 candidate list, that’s 50 separate transformer inference calls per query. Every single time.
Finding 3: ArguAna Breaks Every Assumption
ArguAna is structurally different from every other dataset here. The task is counter-argument retrieval - given an argument, find its rebuttal. This means the query and the relevant document are semantically opposed, not similar.
Dense retrieval assumes similar vectors mean relevant documents. That assumption breaks completely here. This is why all configs perform worse on ArguAna, and why the performance gap between configs is much narrower - no retrieval strategy has a structural advantage when the relevance signal is adversarial.
This is worth keeping in mind if you’re building retrieval for debate, fact-checking, or red-teaming applications. Standard benchmarks won’t prepare you for this.
The Most Interesting Finding: Query Difficulty Changes Everything
This is the analysis I’m most proud of, and the one that has the most practical implications.
The idea: aggregated metrics like average MRR hide a lot. A retrieval config that looks mediocre on average might be the best choice for hard queries, while a cheaper config is perfectly sufficient for easy ones.
How difficulty is defined: I used BM25 per-query MRR from config A as a proxy. If BM25 alone retrieves the right document, the query has strong lexical signal - it’s easy. If BM25 fails, the query requires semantic understanding - it’s hard. Queries are split into three equal terciles per dataset (easy, medium, hard) and all 6 configs are evaluated separately within each bucket.
For hard queries, you need the full pipeline. The cross-encoder catches subtle relevance distinctions that embedding similarity misses entirely. For easy queries, the lexical signal is strong enough that non-reranked hybrid is sufficient - and reranking adds latency with no quality return.
The Practical Implication: Adaptive Retrieval Routing
This finding directly suggests a production system design: don’t pick one config globally. Route queries based on predicted difficulty.
Incoming query │ ▼ Difficulty classifier (lightweight BM25 score proxy) │ ├── Easy query ──► Config C (~194ms, non-reranked hybrid) │ └── Hard query ──► Config E (~867ms, reranked hybrid)
Easy queries get the fast, cheap path. Hard queries get the expensive but accurate path. You avoid paying the 3.5x latency cost of reranking on queries that don’t need it.
This is an active research direction called adaptive retrieval, and the bucketing results here provide quantitative justification for it across four real-world domains.
What I’d Do Differently
A few honest reflections:
Fusion weight tuning matters more than I expected. The gap between B and C on FiQA suggests that hybrid retrieval with poorly tuned weights can actively hurt performance. A per-domain weight search would be a meaningful extension.
The difficulty classifier could be learned. Right now, difficulty is defined post-hoc using BM25 scores. A lightweight query classifier trained to predict difficulty upfront - before retrieval - would make the adaptive routing policy actually deployable.
More adversarial domains needed. ArguAna showed that standard retrieval assumptions can break entirely. Adding more structurally unusual domains (e.g., legal counter-argument, debate) would stress-test these findings further.

Takeaways
1.
Dense-only beats hybrid on semantic domains - don’t default to hybrid without testing.
2.
Reranking is worth it on technical domains, not universally - 7.5% average MRR gain at 3.5x latency cost.
3.
Query difficulty determines the optimal config, not just domain - hard queries need reranking, easy ones don’t.
4.
Adaptive routing is the practical path forward - route by difficulty, not by a single global config choice.
5.
Late chunking is expensive and domain-specific - validate before deploying.
