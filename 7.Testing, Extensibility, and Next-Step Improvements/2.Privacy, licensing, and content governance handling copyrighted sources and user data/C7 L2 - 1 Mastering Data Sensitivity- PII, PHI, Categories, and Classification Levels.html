Privacy Engineering: Sensitive Data
A basic review of what data sensitivity is, its common types, and its classification.
The reason the privacy engineering role exists is due to users’ need for the system to properly manage their information. Users need to know that their privacy is protected within the computer products they use, and the lack of it leads them to distrust and eventually cut ties with the product.
What Are Sensitive Data?
Not all information that users provide to the system must be treated with the same importance; not all of it is necessarily related to their privacy from a legal standpoint that generates the backup[1].
So, data from a person that is considered private by a regulation or the law, and for which privacy mechanisms must be applied by the systems that store and use it, is called sensitive data.
All information may require legal justification for its use and management; and even more so for sensitive data that a system may store and hold.
Sensitive Data: PII
Sensitive data can be data of a person that defines an identity distinct from another[5]; this is called personally identifiable information or PII for its English acronym. Although this is not a legal term[2] but rather a business term. Each regulation may define its own interpretation of PII, such as the CCPA and GDPR[3][7].
In the case of government identities, this can be very broad: passport number, driver’s license ID, insurance number, tax number, among others that the country specifies. The same applies to business identifications: bank account number or patient ID number in a clinic.
There are also other types of data that can be taken into consideration, as they help narrow down the group of people when combined, in order to identify a specific person. For this reason, it is also known as non-sensitive PII[6]; and regulations can define its scope[2].
This list is not definitive and can vary by industry depending on the information they have. For example, some pilots’ unions claim that flight numbers should be considered PII because they can identify pilots on the aircraft, and misuse of that information could violate pilots’ privacy[8].
Sensitive Data: PHI
Another source of sensitive data is that related to health. Things like allergies or diagnoses are considered personal and private.
•
Vaccination.
•
Surgeries and insurance information.
•
Diseases, diagnoses, and treatments.
•
Blood type and clinical results.
It may be that the regulation overlaps the list of sensitive data with other points such as geographic information, social security number, or email, for example[1].
And Other Sensitive Data and Their Categorization
So far, we’ve seen the most common sensitive data that virtually any industry might use; but this is because they are more general in practice.
There are regulations that can be more specific in defining what is considered sensitive data and how to protect it, such as FERPA for academic records[10].
In general, data—both sensitive and non-sensitive—can be categorized into the following groups:
•
Internal Information: Knowledge and beliefs related to the user. Example: interests or authentication methods (password, PIN number)
•
Financial Information: Matters related to the user’s finances. Example: credit information, account or property info, etc.
•
External Information: Used to authenticate the user. Example: sexual, race, governmental, demographic, physical, health, etc.
•
Social Information: Related to interactions with people and organizations. Example: criminal, professional, public life, family, social or communication, etc.
•
Tracking Information: Associated with online or electronic activities and communication. Example: IP address, MAC, physical, location, browsing behavior, etc.
Data Sensitivity Classification
Data sensitivity can be categorized according to the type of sensitivity, and this can be determined by regulations and the industry[9]. It may be categorized into 4 or 3 groups depending on the source[13][14]; but for this post, it is classified as follows.
•
Public: This represents no risk or, in the worst case, a very minor risk[14]. Generally, the information is accessible to anyone without any restrictions[12].
•
Sensitive: Subject to a confidentiality contract and does not require any government regulation[14]. Violating it can cause moderate harm. It may include civil liability[12].
•
Restricted: Disclosure of this information can cause harm, and it is subject to general government regulations[14]. It may include criminal/penal and civil liability[12].
•
Highly Restricted: This is information that requires specific regulations on how to protect it; with very limited use and exposure only with prior explicit authorization[14]. It may include criminal/penal and civil liability[12].
Conclusions
User information can be categorized as sensitive data and classified by intensity. The key to understanding within the business which data are sensitive depends on the laws and regulations in each country.
It is also possible that there are data that are not private under a regulation but that ethically fall into the same category according to the cultural norms of the moment in the country using the system. The data engineer helps determine this point so that the organization has privacy as an element in its brand.
While many groups of sensitive data have a well-defined trend, they eventually share certain common information, thanks to the regulation that defines it[4][1].
