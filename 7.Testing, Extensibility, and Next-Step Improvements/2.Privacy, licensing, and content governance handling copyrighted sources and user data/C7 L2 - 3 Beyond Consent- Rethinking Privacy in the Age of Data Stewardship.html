Title: Rethinking Consent: Data, Privacy, and the Problem of Entropy
Introduction
I’m going to warn you up front: there are no audio visuals in this talk. Part of that is because if you’re looking at someone, I want you to be looking at me. Part of it is that after thirty years in technology, I don’t trust technology. No slides, no cards—just a conversation.
When I heard the topic was entropy, the first image that came to mind was data. We are surrounded by data that seems to be falling out of control: corporations losing it, government agencies having it stolen, and vast quantities we voluntarily supply being collected and analyzed every day.
The scale is almost meaningless to most people. Talking about petabytes and terabytes becomes abstract unless you’re a computer scientist. Yet the sheer volume of this data has a tremendous impact on privacy—especially personal data and how it’s used, inferred, and traded.
The State of Personal Data
We post pictures of our meals, send millions of texts per second, and upload images and videos at rates previously unimaginable. Much of this activity is voluntary, yet it feeds systems that buy, sell, and infer increasingly intimate profiles about us.
Some of the data about you may not even exist in a direct sense—rather, it’s constructed or inferred: are you a good credit risk, should you be marketed to, or are you likely to default? These are often algorithmic inferences that may never match any objective record about you.
A startling statistic: the New York Times reported in 2017 that a single company most people have never heard of engages in fifty trillion personal data transactions a year. Fifty trillion. That buying and selling of personal data feels completely out of control—and along with it, our privacy.
Consent as the Dominant Paradigm
One of the largest drivers of current privacy frameworks is consent. Privacy law as we know it traces back to the 1960s and an academic study by Dr. Alan Westin at Columbia University. Westin’s work defined privacy as the claim of individuals, groups, or institutions to determine for themselves when, how, and to what extent information about them is communicated to others.
This definition was widely embraced. William Safire captured the sentiment in the New York Times, saying that, with certain legitimate exceptions for law enforcement and public interest, control of information must rest with the person himself.
The U.S. Supreme Court echoed the idea: in Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee (1988), privacy was defined to include an individual’s control of information concerning his or her person. Globally, countries have adopted similar concepts.
Consent in Modern Regulations
Europe’s GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)—effective widely since May 2018—explicitly shapes data protection law. While regulators there argue they have not made the U.S. mistake of over-relying on consent, GDPR still uses the term “consent” over one hundred times.
California’s California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) also builds on the idea that individuals should have a right to control how personal data gathered online is used. On its face, consent sounds unassailable—who could object to a person controlling their own data?
Yet, I want to outline why focusing so heavily on consent is both impractical and undesirable. I’ll give seven quick reasons why consent, as the center of privacy protection, is failing us.
1)
Complexity of Privacy Notices
Privacy notices are everywhere, and almost nobody reads them. They’re long and written in complex legalese. For example, PayPal’s privacy notice clocks in at 36,275 words—longer than Hamlet.
Another popular company’s privacy policy measured 19,972 words—longer than Macbeth. A 2008 study estimated that reading the privacy policies of the 40–45 most popular websites would take an individual the equivalent of 30 full working days per year.
These documents are complex because the subject matter is complex. Most people simply can’t or won’t wade through them.
2)
Inaccessibility in Many Contexts
Consent becomes effectively impossible in many real-world situations. How do you provide informed consent in group settings? For instance, if everyone in this audience could be recording right now, did anyone consent to being recorded?
When you walk down a city street with CCTV cameras, you didn’t sign anything. When data is inferred about you as part of a crowd, individual consent is impractical or meaningless.
3)
People Ignore Consent Notices
Consent has proven empirically ineffective because people simply ignore these notices. Even regulators acknowledge this. FTC Chairmen have openly noted that consumers don’t read privacy policies.
One former FTC Chair described a law that produced “acres of trees” worth of barely comprehensible privacy notices. All those notices don’t translate into meaningful understanding.
4)
Illusory Consent
A lot of consent is not real choice. Consider software updates: to update your iPhone, you’re presented with pages of terms and privacy text. You can agree—or you cannot use the device properly.
This coercive structure makes consent illusory. If your alternatives are “agree” or “we’ll brick your expensive device,” then the agreement isn’t freely given.
5)
Consent Shifts Burdens to Individuals
Consent is often framed as a right, but in practice it becomes a duty—a legal burden we bear when interacting with services. When you click “I agree,” you may be signing away protections and shifting liability.
Think of a parking garage ticket with legal terms on the back: it might appear as a contract or a right to park, but really it’s a shift of risk onto you. Consent often functions the same way.
6)
Consent Works Against Public Interest
Consent, when treated as inviolable, can harm collective and public interests. Certain activities—press coverage, fraud prevention, crime detection—cannot realistically wait for individual consent.
We frequently override consent in these contexts because the social value of using data for public safety, journalism, or fraud prevention outweighs rigid individual consent requirements.
7)
Consent Leads to Weak Privacy Protection
Most critically, over-reliance on consent can produce poor privacy outcomes. Clicking “I agree” frequently results in giving away broad, often unlimited rights to data use.
Imagine consumer protection law allowing vendors to ask permission to defraud customers: we would not permit that. Yet current practices effectively let companies ask users to consent away important protections.
Toward a Better Model: Stewardship, Defaults, and Redress
I don’t want to end with only criticism. Here are four constructive ideas that could lead to a healthier privacy regime.
1)
Focus on Stewardship, Not Evasion
Instead of giving collectors the power to offload liability via consent, hold data collectors accountable as stewards of the data. If you collect and use someone’s data, you should be responsible for harms that arise from misuse or breaches.
We treat other professions—lawyers, bankers, doctors—as fiduciaries or trusted professionals. Why not require similar stewardship when organizations handle the most personal information about people?
2)
Define Permitted Uses and Defaults
We could identify categories of generally permissible uses and categories that are forbidden. Stalking and fraud are obviously out. Routine activities like bill collection, fraud detection, and well-governed research might be allowed under clear security and governance standards.
By creating sensible defaults for routine uses, we reduce the burden of constant consent prompts while protecting against misuse.
3)
Strengthen Redress Mechanisms
Entropy guarantees that things will go wrong. Data breaches, misuse, or mistaken inferences will inevitably occur. Right now, many people learn about problems from the news rather than from the responsible party.
We need better mechanisms for timely redress: transparent notification, practical remedies, and accessible enforcement so individuals are not left defenseless.
4)
Make Consent Meaningful When It Matters
When consent is used, it should be meaningful, timely, and effective. Rather than presenting a wall of legalese at device setup, give people context-sensitive, just-in-time prompts.
A good example: a location permission prompt that appears when an app is actually using your location. That’s useful and actionable—you can make a real choice and change settings on the spot.
Conclusion
Consent has its place, but it cannot be the linchpin of a modern privacy regime. It is impractical, often illusory, and can serve as a shield for poor privacy practices.
By shifting toward stewardship, clear permitted-use rules, stronger redress, and better-designed consent interactions, we can build a system that respects individual autonomy without handing responsibility off to the person who least can bear it.
Thank you very much.
